USB-C Chargers & Wattage, Explained

"65W fast charger" on a box sounds like a single, comparable spec. It isn't. Wattage is a ceiling, not a guarantee — the actual charging speed your device gets depends on a negotiation between the charger, the cable, and the device, and any one of the three can be the bottleneck.

1. Wattage is per-port, and it's shared

A charger labeled "100W" with two or three ports usually cannot deliver 100W to each port at once — that number is the combined maximum across all ports, split according to a priority scheme the manufacturer sets. If you plan to charge two devices simultaneously, check the per-port wattage breakdown (usually in the fine print or manual), not just the headline number.

2. Your device has its own ceiling

A phone that supports 20W fast charging will not charge any faster on a 65W charger than it would on a 20W one — the device negotiates down to what it can accept. The higher-wattage charger is still useful if you'll ever plug in a laptop or tablet that needs more, but it buys you nothing extra for that phone specifically.

3. The cable matters as much as the charger

A USB-C cable that isn't rated for the wattage you're pushing through it will silently cap the charging speed — often without any error or warning. Cables rated for higher power (look for "100W" or "5A" on USB-C cables meant for laptop charging) cost more but are necessary if you're charging a high-power laptop; a cheap or older cable can bottleneck even a great charger.

4. USB Power Delivery (PD) vs. proprietary fast charging

USB-PD is the open, cross-brand standard most modern phones, tablets, and laptops use for fast charging over USB-C. Some phone brands additionally support their own proprietary fast-charging protocol that goes faster than PD, but only with that brand's own charger and cable — a universal PD charger will still fast-charge the phone, just not always at the brand's fastest advertised speed.

Quick framework

  • Charging one phone only — Match the charger's wattage to your phone's actual maximum fast-charge spec (check your phone's manual — going higher wastes money).
  • Charging a laptop — Check your laptop's charging wattage requirement first (often 45–100W) and buy a charger and cable both rated at or above it.
  • Charging multiple devices from one charger — Check the per-port wattage table, not just the total; a device plugged into the "wrong" port on a multi-port charger can charge noticeably slower.
  • Traveling — A single higher-wattage PD charger with multiple ports usually beats carrying several single-purpose chargers, as long as you check the per-port split above.

The honest summary: buy a charger rated at or slightly above what your most demanding device needs, buy a cable rated for that same wattage, and treat any number higher than that as headroom for a future device rather than a speed boost for your current one.
